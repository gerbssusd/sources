First, it builds your own credibility as a writer — it shows your reader that you've actually done the research, and that you're approaching your topic honestly.

And third, it's a gift to your reader. Every source you cite is a resource you've already found and vetted. If someone wants to learn more about your topic, your citations are a ready-made reading list.
-->

For college-level work, "citing your sources" really means two things working together.

The first is in-text or parenthetical citation — a short signal, right inside your sentences, that tells your reader "this next bit came from somewhere else."

The second is a Works Cited page — or in APA, a References page — at the end of your paper, where each of those sources gets a full, complete entry so a reader could go find it themselves.

The exact format changes a bit depending on your discipline. Your writing classes, and a lot of other humanities classes, use MLA — the Modern Language Association format. A psychology class is more likely to use APA. Everything we're covering today is MLA, since that's what you'll use most in this course.

<!--
Here's the core rule: every single time you use an idea or language from a source — whether you're quoting it directly, summarizing it, or paraphrasing it — you need an in-text citation.

Sometimes that's as simple as naming the author in your own sentence. Other times you'll add a parenthetical citation — a citation inside parentheses, in the text of your essay.

And here's why this matters so much: if you don't mark that material as coming from a source, your reader has no way of knowing it. They'll assume the words and ideas are yours. Since they aren't, that's plagiarism — even if it was accidental.

Let's look at the three specific ways you can identify a source in your writing.
-->

<!-- ================= SLIDE 5: METHOD 1 ================= -->

<div class="kicker" style="color: #C9A227;">Method 1 of 3</div>
<h1 class="heading mt-2 text-4xl" style="color: #1E2761;">Introduce the Author or Title</h1>

<div class="grid grid-cols-2 gap-8 mt-8">
  <div>
    <div class="icon-circle w-14 h-14" style="background: #B5502E;">
      <img src="/icons/UserCheck_white.png" />
    </div>
    <p class="text-base mt-4" style="color: #242424;">Name the author or the source's title directly in your sentence so the reader knows right away this idea isn't yours.</p>
    <p class="text-sm italic mt-4" style="color: #5A5A5A;">First mention: full name ("According to Jonas Fogbottom . . .")<br>Later mentions: last name only ("Fogbottom goes on to . . .")</p>
  </div>
  <div class="card p-6" style="background: #F4EFE6; border-color: #DDD3BE;">
    <div class="kicker text-xs" style="color: #B5502E;">Example</div>
    <p class="italic text-base mt-4" style="color: #242424;">In the article, "Grooming Poodles for Fun and Profit," <b style="color: #1E2761; font-style: normal;">Jonas Fogbottom</b> explains, "Poodle grooming is a labor of love. It takes years of practice to be good at it, but once learned, it's a fun and worthwhile career."</p>
    <p class="text-xs mt-4" style="color: #B5502E;">Author + title are introduced — no parenthetical citation needed here.</p>
  </div>
</div>

<!--
Method one: introduce the author and the title.

By naming the author, or the title of the piece, right in your own sentence, you make it obvious to your reader that what follows is from a source — not your own original idea.

Here's Jonas Fogbottom's example: "In the article, 'Grooming Poodles for Fun and Profit,' Jonas Fogbottom explains, 'Poodle grooming is a labor of love...'" Notice the author and the title are both right there in the sentence.

One quick rule: the first time you mention a source, use the speaker's full name. If you come back to that source later in the paper, you can just use their last name — "Fogbottom goes on to discuss..." And remember, the "speaker" isn't always the author of the article — sometimes a writer quotes someone else, and you're citing that person's words.
-->

<h1 class="heading mt-2 text-4xl" style="color: #1E2761;">Use Linking (Attributive) Language</h1>

<div class="grid grid-cols-2 gap-8 mt-8">
  <div>
    <div class="icon-circle w-14 h-14" style="background: #1E2761;">
      <img src="/icons/Link2_white.png" />

    <p class="text-base mt-4" style="color: #242424;">Signal phrases remind your reader you're still talking about the same source — useful across several sentences in a row.</p>
    <div class="mt-6 flex flex-col gap-2">
      <div class="rounded-full px-4 py-2 text-sm italic" style="border: 1px solid #1E2761; color: #1E2761;">The author also explains . . .</div>

      <div class="rounded-full px-4 py-2 text-sm italic" style="border: 1px solid #1E2761; color: #1E2761;">The article goes on to say . . .</div>
      <div class="rounded-full px-4 py-2 text-sm italic" style="border: 1px solid #1E2761; color: #1E2761;">The data set also demonstrates . . .</div>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="card p-6">
    <div class="kicker text-xs" style="color: #B5502E;">Example</div>
    <p class="text-sm mt-4" style="color: #242424;">In the article, "Grooming Poodles for Fun and Profit," Jonas Fogbottom says that although it takes a long time to become a skilled poodle groomer, it's well worth the effort and leads to a good career. <b style="color: #B5502E;">Fogbottom goes on to explain</b> how one is trained in the art of dog grooming. <b style="color: #B5502E;">The article also gives</b> a set of resources for people who want to know more.</p>
  </div>
</div>

<!--
Method two: linking, or attributive, language.

This is language that reminds your reader you're still talking about the same source you just introduced — phrases like "the author also explains," "Fogbottom continues," "the article goes on to say," or "the data set also demonstrates."

Look at the extended Fogbottom example on the right: after that first sentence, we add "Fogbottom goes on to explain..." and "The article also gives..." Those linking phrases make it crystal clear we're still pulling from the same source, without having to reintroduce it from scratch every sentence.

You'll use this kind of language throughout any researched essay — alongside parenthetical citations, or sometimes instead of them.
-->

<div class="flex items-start gap-4 mt-8">
  <div class="icon-circle w-14 h-14" style="background: #B5502E;">
    <img src="/icons/Parentheses_white.png" />

<!--
Method three, and probably the one you'll use most: the parenthetical citation. That's just a citation enclosed in parentheses.

The classic version includes the author's last name and a page number, if there is one — for example, (Smith 41). On screen you can see the Fogbottom quote — since this source has no page numbers, we just use his last name in parentheses at the end.

Here's a pro tip from the original text, and it's a good one: whatever comes first in your Works Cited entry is what goes inside your parentheses — almost always the author's last name. That's a great argument for building your Works Cited entry the moment you start using a source, instead of waiting until the end of your paper.

One formatting note that trips up a lot of writers: the period goes after the closing parenthesis, not before it. Watch that when you proofread.
-->

<!-- ================= SLIDE 8: PARENTHETICAL VARIATIONS ================= -->

<div class="kicker" style="color: #C9A227;">Parenthetical Citations</div>
<h1 class="heading mt-2 text-4xl" style="color: #1E2761;">Common Variations You'll Need</h1>

<div class="mt-8 flex flex-col gap-2">
  <div class="card flex items-center justify-between px-6 py-3">
    <span class="text-base" style="color: #242424;">One author</span>
    <span class="rounded-lg px-4 py-1 font-bold text-sm" style="background: #1E2761; color: #C9A227;">(Fogbottom 16)</span>
  </div>
  <div class="card flex items-center justify-between px-6 py-3">
    <span class="text-base" style="color: #242424;">Two authors</span>
    <span class="rounded-lg px-4 py-1 font-bold text-sm" style="background: #1E2761; color: #C9A227;">(Smith and Jones 24)</span>
  </div>
  <div class="card flex items-center justify-between px-6 py-3">
    <span class="text-base" style="color: #242424;">Three or more authors</span>
    <span class="rounded-lg px-4 py-1 font-bold text-sm" style="background: #1E2761; color: #C9A227;">(Smith et al. 62)</span>
  </div>
  <div class="card flex items-center justify-between px-6 py-3">
    <span class="text-base" style="color: #242424;">No page number available</span>
    <span class="rounded-lg px-4 py-1 font-bold text-sm" style="background: #1E2761; color: #C9A227;">(Fogbottom)</span>
  </div>
  <div class="card flex items-center justify-between px-6 py-3">
    <span class="text-base" style="color: #242424;">Quoted inside another source</span>
    <span class="rounded-lg px-4 py-1 font-bold text-sm" style="background: #1E2761; color: #C9A227;">(qtd. in Fogbottom)</span>
  </div>
</div>

<!--
Parenthetical citations flex a little depending on your source. Here are the patterns you'll use most:

One author: last name and page number — (Fogbottom 16).

Two authors: list both — (Smith and Jones 24).

Three or more authors: list just the first author, followed by "et al.," which is Latin for "and others" — (Smith et al. 62).

No page number — common with web sources or anything electronic: just drop the page number — (Fogbottom).

And last: if you're quoting someone who was cited inside another source — say, you want to quote Smith, but you only found that quote inside an article by Fogbottom — you write "qtd. in Fogbottom," meaning "quoted in Fogbottom." That tells your reader exactly where you actually found the words.
-->

<!--
A few formatting rules for titles that show up constantly in MLA work.

Periodical and book titles are always italicized. Article and chapter titles are never italicized — they go in quotation marks instead.
